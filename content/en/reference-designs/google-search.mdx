<Step id="scale-driver" title="What drives the scale">

Brin and Page’s 1998 paper describes a prototype with at least 24 million indexed pages. That historical corpus is a useful search-design anchor, not a description of modern Google’s index or query rate.

This **illustrative** graph chooses 500,000 queries/s globally and fixed 32 KiB results. Each regional query coordinator scatters to two mandatory index shards, one in each region, waits for both, calls a European final ranker, then fetches document data from US object storage.

The all-branches join makes the slower shard part of every query’s path. Region propagation and later ranking/document calls accumulate even when shard CPU is cheap. Index freshness, relevance, crawling and ranking quality are not established by this capacity simulation. All queries are reads, so celebrity key concentration does not model hot-row write locks.

[Public scale anchor](https://research.google/pubs/the-anatomy-of-a-large-scale-hypertextual-web-search-engine/). All other rates, byte sizes, service times, prices and capacities are illustrative assumptions in the reference design.

<Step id="test-remedy" title="Failure ladder and what to try">

**Observed Failure ladder.** At 1x, global timely-success is already below the ladder’s 99% threshold. At 4x, both admission-to-query-coordinator edges saturate. These edge rungs also expose the engine’s exchange-rate ceiling, rather than establishing Google’s query-network capacity.

These are the first three rungs of an independent normal-day sweep at **1x, 4x and 16x**, with 2 s warm-up and 8 s measurement per level, fixed 250 ms steps and no sweep drain. Levels report **unstable — no steady state** because the observation window is shorter than the required 30 s. The engine floors edge service time at one microsecond, limiting an edge to roughly one million exchanges/s even when its configured bandwidth permits more. Treat the ladder as a diagnostic for this model.

**What to try.** Try co-locating the final ranker with a coordinator, then compare latency and timely-success under the same scenario. Test index-0’s brownout to expose the all-branches dependency. A partial-result or quorum experiment changes the product’s completeness requirement and needs an explicit lesson assumption; Scale cannot grade relevance. Remedies are suggestions to test; none is verified here.

The six Scale scenarios are normal day, peak event (4x), US region outage, hot key/celebrity (4x concentrated demand), dependency brownout (10x service time) and DDoS (50x application traffic). Disturbances run from second 4 through second 8; celebrity concentration applies from the start. The outage retains each client's regional entry, with no automatic evacuation. DDoS adds competing traffic without identifying legitimate users.

When sandbox integration is available, select this reference and its normal-day pack in Scale mode, copy it, change one budget, and compare against the unchanged scenario. Check fidelity reasons before reading a disabled metric; correctness stays **unknown**.
