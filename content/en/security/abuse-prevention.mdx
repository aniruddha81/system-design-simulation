<Step id="abuse" title="Attacks that use the product as designed">

Abuse is not a bug in your code. It is your login, sign-up, search and checkout used **at scale with bad intent**: testing leaked passwords, creating fake accounts for spam or promotions, scraping prices, testing stolen cards with small purchases. OWASP's catalogue of automated threats names each of these patterns. Every request is individually valid, so the signal is in the **pattern**.

<Step id="stuffing" title="Credential stuffing beats per-IP limits">

**Credential stuffing** replays username and password pairs leaked from other sites, betting on password reuse. Attackers rent residential proxy networks with very large pools of IP addresses, so a per-IP limit that would stop one machine barely slows a distributed attack. Each IP looks like one household making a few login attempts.

<Step id="signals" title="Layer the signals">

No single signal holds. Effective defences stack several that are expensive for an attacker to fake at once.

<Step id="false-positives" title="The base-rate trap">

At scale, most traffic is legitimate. A classifier that wrongly blocks "only" 1% of good logins can still produce more wrongful blocks than correct ones when abuse is a small share of the traffic. Those wrongly blocked users raise support tickets, churn, and learn to distrust your security. Always compute the share of blocks that are false, not just the false-positive rate.

<Step id="friction" title="Graduated friction">

Because every signal is imperfect, respond in proportion to risk instead of a binary allow or block. Low risk passes silently; medium risk gets a step-up such as an email confirmation, an MFA prompt or a challenge; high risk is blocked or held for review. Do not tell the attacker which signal fired: precise feedback is a free tuning tool for them.
