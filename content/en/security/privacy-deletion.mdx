<Step id="minimise" title="The safest data is data you never kept">

Every field you store is something to secure, back up, disclose on request and eventually delete. The GDPR writes this into its principles: **data minimisation** (collect only what is necessary for the purpose, Article 5(1)(c)), **storage limitation** (keep it identifiable no longer than needed, Article 5(1)(e)), and **data protection by design and by default** (Article 25). Good engineering arrives at the same place for a simpler reason: a breach can only leak what exists.

<Step id="copies" title="Find every copy">

A user's row in the primary database is only the start. A deletion request must reach every place their data was copied to, and in most systems nobody has the full list until someone writes it down.

<Step id="pipeline" title="A deletion pipeline">

Treat deletion as a first-class workflow with an owner, an audit trail and a deadline. Under the GDPR, a controller must act on a request without undue delay and within one month, extendable by two further months for complex cases (Article 12(3)).

<Step id="backups" title="Backups and the last copy">

Backups are usually immutable, by design. Rewriting them to remove one user defeats their purpose. The common approach is to keep backup retention short and documented, make sure a restored backup re-applies outstanding deletions before it serves traffic, and treat erasure as complete when the last copy expires. So the real deletion time is processing time plus the longest retention of any copy you do not purge on request.

<Step id="crypto-shred" title="Crypto-shredding">

**Crypto-shredding** encrypts each user's data with a per-user key held in a key store. To erase the user, destroy their key: every copy encrypted under it, including those in immutable backups, becomes unreadable at once. NIST SP 800-88 recognises cryptographic erase as a sanitisation method when the key is reliably destroyed.

It works only for data that was actually encrypted under that key. Derived data, plaintext in logs, data shared with processors and copies of the key itself in key-store backups all escape it.
