<Step id="when-to-retry" title="Retries fix transient failures, and only those">

A retry is a bet that the failure was transient: a dropped packet, a leader election in progress, a server that restarted. Against those, one quick retry turns a visible error into a slightly slower success. Against a persistent failure, such as an overloaded or broken dependency, a retry is just more load on the thing that is already failing.

So classify the error before retrying. A 400 Bad Request or 403 Forbidden will fail identically every time. A 503 or 429 that says "slow down" deserves a retry only after waiting, and only within a budget. And a retry of a non-idempotent operation after a timeout risks doing it twice.

<Step id="amplification" title="Retries multiply across layers">

The trap is that every layer of a system retries independently. A client retries the API, the API retries the service, the service retries the database. Each layer is reasonable on its own; together they multiply. With 3 attempts at each of 4 layers, one user request becomes 3⁴ = 81 attempts on the database, arriving precisely when it is least able to cope.

This is the **retry storm**. It turns a brief overload into a sustained one: the dependency recovers a little, gets flooded by retried traffic, falls over again. Google's SRE book recommends retrying at one layer only, typically just above the failing dependency, and having the other layers fail fast.

<Step id="backoff" title="Exponential backoff with a cap">

Retrying immediately repeats the failure at full speed. **Exponential backoff** waits longer after each failure: base, 2 × base, 4 × base and so on, giving the dependency room to recover. A **cap** stops waits from growing absurd: without one, the tenth retry with a 100 ms base would wait 51 seconds.

Backoff alone also bounds how fast one client can hammer a server. It does not bound how many clients do it at once.

<Step id="jitter" title="Jitter spreads the herd">

If a thousand clients fail at the same moment, as they do when a shared dependency blips, plain exponential backoff makes them all retry at the same moments too: at 100 ms, 200 ms, 400 ms. Each retry wave is a synchronised spike. Brooker's 2015 simulations at AWS compared strategies and found that adding randomness, **jitter**, removes the spikes and cuts total work substantially.

**Full jitter** sleeps a uniformly random time between 0 and the capped exponential value. It spreads retries evenly across the window. The trade-off is a little more variance for any one client in exchange for far less contention across all of them.

<Step id="budget" title="Retry budgets bound the worst case">

Backoff and jitter shape *when* retries arrive. A **retry budget** caps *how many*. The client tracks the ratio of retries to first attempts and stops retrying once retries exceed a fraction, commonly around 10%. The SRE book describes both a per-request limit and a per-client budget of this kind.

When things are healthy, the budget is never reached and transient failures are still retried. When a dependency is truly failing, total load rises by at most 10% instead of multiplying, and the budget turns retries off precisely when they are useless.

<Step id="storm-story" title="Anatomy of a retry storm">

A database slows down for 30 seconds during a failover. Client requests time out, and every layer retries. Load on the database jumps to several times normal. The database finishes failing over and is healthy, but now faces a traffic level it was never sized for, so it stays slow, which keeps the timeouts and retries coming. The original cause is gone, yet the outage continues. That self-sustaining state is a **metastable failure**, covered in the reliability track.
