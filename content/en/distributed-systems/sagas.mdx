<Step id="the-problem" title="One business action, several databases">

Booking a trip reserves a flight, a hotel room and a car, each owned by a different service with its own database. The business wants all three or none. Inside one database, a transaction gives you that. Across services, there is no shared transaction, and naive sequencing leaves you with a flight and no hotel when the second call fails.

There are two broad answers. **Atomic commit** protocols such as two-phase commit make all participants commit or abort together. **Sagas** give up atomicity and isolation, commit each step locally, and undo with compensations when a later step fails.

<Step id="two-pc" title="Two-phase commit">

In 2PC a **coordinator** asks every participant to **prepare**. A participant that votes yes durably records that it can commit, holding its locks, and promises to obey the coordinator's decision. If every vote is yes, the coordinator durably records **commit** and tells everyone; any no vote, or a missing vote, means **abort**. Participants then acknowledge so the coordinator can forget the transaction.

The cost is at least two round trips and several forced log writes on the commit path, with locks held throughout. Databases use 2PC internally for distributed transactions, and the XA standard exposes it across vendors.

<Step id="blocking" title="Why 2PC blocks">

The weak point is the window after a participant votes yes. It has promised to commit if told to, so it cannot abort on its own; it also cannot commit, because another participant may have voted no. If the coordinator crashes now, the participant must hold its locks and wait until the coordinator recovers. Its rows are unavailable to everyone else in the meantime.

That is why 2PC is called a **blocking** protocol. Gray and Lamport (2006) showed that replicating the coordinator's decision with Paxos removes the single point of blocking; systems like Spanner run 2PC over Paxos-replicated groups for exactly this reason.

Keep Transaction protocol set to **Two-phase commit** and select **coordinator crash after prepare** in Script. Watch prepared participants retain their state when the coordinator goes offline.

<Step id="saga" title="Sagas: local commits and compensations">

Garcia-Molina and Salem introduced **sagas** in 1987 for long-lived transactions that would hold locks far too long under 2PC. A saga is a sequence of local transactions T1, T2 … Tn, each with a **compensating transaction** C1, C2 … that semantically undoes it. If Tk fails, the saga runs C(k−1) down to C1.

Compensation is semantic, not a rollback: you cannot un-send an email, but you can send a correction; you cannot erase a charge, but you can refund it. That gives sagas their two famous weaknesses: other transactions can see intermediate states (no isolation), and some steps cannot be compensated at all.

<Step id="compensate" title="Order the compensations, place the irreversible step last">

Compensations run in reverse order, so each undo sees the state its forward step left. Steps that cannot be undone, such as shipping a parcel or sending a wire transfer that cannot be recalled, should be the **pivot**: placed after every step that might still fail, so that once the pivot commits, the remaining steps are ones that can be retried until they succeed.

Set Transaction protocol to **Saga** and select **saga failure and reverse compensation** in Script. Follow the completed steps and the reverse compensation path after the next step fails.

<Step id="outbox" title="The dual-write problem and the outbox">

Every saga step must do two things: commit its local change and tell the next step, usually by publishing a message. Writing to a database and to a broker are two separate systems. Commit then publish, and a crash in between loses the message; publish then commit, and a failed commit leaves a message about something that never happened.

The **transactional outbox** writes the message into an outbox table in the *same* local transaction as the business change. A separate relay reads the outbox and publishes to the broker, marking rows as sent. A crash after publishing but before marking means the relay publishes again, so delivery is at least once and consumers must deduplicate, as in the [idempotency lesson](/learn/distributed-systems/idempotency).

Set Transaction protocol to **Transactional outbox** and select **transactional outbox** in Script. Watch the atomic local write, the relay retry after a crash and the consumer deduplicate the repeated delivery.
