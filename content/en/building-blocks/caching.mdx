<Step id="why-cache" title="A database that cannot keep up">

Your database answers a read in about 5 ms and saturates at a few thousand reads per second. Traffic is growing past that. A cache in front of it answers in under a millisecond, but only for keys it already holds.

The number that matters is the **hit rate**: the share of reads the cache answers. Every miss still goes to the database.

</Step>

<Step id="db-load" title="Predict the database load">

With cache-aside, only misses reach the database:

**database reads per second = request rate × (1 − hit rate)**

That is why hit rate improvements near the top matter so much: going from 98% to 99% halves the database load.

</Step>

<Step id="read-path" title="Follow a miss through cache-aside">

In **cache-aside** (also called lazy loading) the application owns the cache. The cache never talks to the database. On a hit, the service returns the cached value. On a miss, the service does the work and fills the cache itself.

<Step id="skew" title="Where the hit rate comes from">

A cache does not create hits. **Key popularity does.** Web and application traffic is highly skewed: a few keys get most of the reads. Breslau et al. measured that request frequency roughly follows a Zipf law, where the key of rank *i* gets a share proportional to 1 / *i*.

Under Zipf with exponent 1, the top *k* of *N* keys receive about **H(k) ÷ H(N)** of all requests, where H(n) ≈ ln n + 0.577. So a cache holding 1% of the keys can serve well over half the reads.

Eviction decides which keys stay. LRU keeps recently used keys; the Che approximation predicts LRU's hit rate from a single "characteristic time" for which keys survive. For skewed, stable popularity, LRU comes close to the ideal of holding the top *k*.

<Step id="freshness" title="Keep cached data fresh">

A cached value is a copy, so it can go stale. You have two levers: a **TTL**, which bounds staleness by time, and **invalidation**, where the writer deletes the key after changing the database. Deleting rather than updating the key avoids races in which two writers leave an older value behind.

<Step id="stampede" title="The stampede after a restart">

A cache node restarts empty. Every hot key misses at once, and every request that misses goes to the database while the first fill is still in flight. The database, sized for a 95% hit rate, suddenly sees close to 100% of the traffic. This is a **stampede**, also called a thundering herd or dogpile.

</Step>

<Step id="stampede-fix" title="Prevent the stampede">

Facebook's memcache paper handles this with **leases**: the first miss for a key gets a token to fill it, and other clients wait briefly and retry the cache instead of hitting the database. In-process, the same idea is called **request coalescing** or single-flight.
