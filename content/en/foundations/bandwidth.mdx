<Step id="units" title="A byte is eight bits">

Network links are commonly sold in bits per second; response sizes are commonly measured in bytes. In this lesson **1 MB = 1,000,000 bytes**, while **1 Mbit = 1,000,000 bits**. A 100 Mbit/s link has an ideal payload ceiling of 12.5 MB/s before protocol overhead.

Do not silently substitute binary units. A MiB is 1,048,576 bytes, and a GiB is 1,073,741,824 bytes. Neither is wrong, but mixing them makes capacity and cost estimates disagree. Write units beside each input and cancel them in the formula.

<Step id="transfer" title="Separate serialization from propagation">

Putting 50 MB onto an idle 100 Mbit/s link takes **50 × 8 / 100 = 4 seconds**. That is serialization time. Propagation, setup, queueing and server work are additional terms. A small response may be dominated by propagation; a large download may be dominated by bytes divided by bandwidth.

Buying a faster link reduces serialization but cannot make a signal travel instantly between continents. Moving a cached copy closer can reduce propagation and the long-haul transfer demand at once. The best intervention depends on which term dominates.

<Step id="load" title="Aggregate traffic saturates a shared link">

At 1,000 responses/s and 50 kB per response, payload demand is **50 MB/s = 400 Mbit/s**. A 100 Mbit/s outbound link cannot carry that sustained rate. More application workers cannot change this ceiling; they can only enqueue bytes faster.

Leave headroom for bursts and overhead. Request and response directions can have different sizes and bottlenecks. A service with tiny GET requests and large images may have low inbound bandwidth while outbound serialization dominates latency.

</Step>

<Step id="queue" title="Bandwidth contention becomes latency">

Suppose ten 10 MB responses share a 10 MB/s FIFO link. The bytes take ten seconds to serialize in total, even if every handler finishes its CPU work immediately. The last response waits behind earlier bytes. A low CPU chart does not imply a fast service.

Bound queued bytes, not just request counts, when payload sizes vary widely. One large export can consume more memory and link time than thousands of metadata responses. Separate bulk traffic or apply fair scheduling when small interactive requests must retain a deadline.

<Step id="compression" title="Compression trades CPU for fewer bytes">

Compression helps when the saved transfer and queueing time exceeds compression cost. Text and repeated JSON often compress well; already compressed video or images may not. Measure size reduction and CPU at realistic concurrency before applying it to every response.

For public versioned static assets, edge caching moves delivery away from the origin. An origin shield can collapse misses from many edges. Cache keys must include representation differences, and private responses need explicit policies to avoid exposing one user's data to another.

<Step id="egress" title="A cache hit changes who sends the bytes">

Cost is **billable GB × price per GB**, with extra terms for requests, storage and other contract details. If a hypothetical agreement charges 8 cents/GB and 10,000 GB leave the origin, that component costs **$800**. This is arithmetic with a supplied price, not a vendor quote.

A CDN can reduce origin egress while still billing for delivery from the edge to the user. Distinguish origin-to-edge traffic, user delivery and cross-region replication. Do not claim a 90% origin cache hit rate removes 90% of all network bills.

<Step id="tradeoff" title="Choose a complete bandwidth intervention">

An origin shield reduces repeated upstream misses but adds another hop on a miss. Compression lowers bytes but can raise compute use. A larger link buys capacity but still needs overload handling. Each decision should preserve freshness, privacy and response-time requirements.

Predict the new byte rate first, then observe link queueing and errors. A change that lowers average egress but synchronizes cache expiry may still create a burst that overwhelms the origin. Use staggered expiry or request coalescing where repeated misses are possible.
