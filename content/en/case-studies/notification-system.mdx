<Step id="requirements" title="Requirements">

Internal services send a notification for a user and a template; the system picks channels (push, SMS, email) from preferences, respects opt-outs, quiet hours and frequency caps, tracks status and retries transient failures.

Non-functional: **accepting a request takes under 50 ms and never depends on provider health**; at-least-once delivery to providers with duplicates suppressed where the channel allows; 2FA codes arrive within seconds while marketing can wait.

<Step id="estimates" title="Estimates">

- **Volume**: 1B notifications/day ≈ **11.6k/s** average; campaigns spike to 10× for minutes.
- **Backlog in a 10-minute provider outage**: 11.6k/s × 600 s ≈ **7M messages**, about 7 GB at 1 KB each. A durable log handles that; an in-memory queue does not.
- **Sender concurrency**: Little's law, 11.6k/s × 40 ms provider latency ≈ **460 requests in flight**.
- **Partitions**: a partition is consumed one message at a time, in order, so you need at least as many partitions as in-flight messages — or unordered consumption.

<Step id="api" title="API">

- `POST /v1/notifications` with `userId`, `template`, `params`, `priority` and an `Idempotency-Key` → **201** queued, **200** duplicate, **429** over quota.
- `GET /v1/notifications/{id}` → status and attempts.
- `PUT /v1/users/{id}/preferences` → channels, quiet hours, opt-outs.

<Step id="data-model" title="Data model">

- **notifications**: ID, user, template, channel, status, attempts, timestamps. Written on accept, updated by senders; about 6 TB for 30 days of status.
- **preferences**: per-user channels, opt-outs and quiet hours. Read on every send, cached aggressively, evaluated **at send time** because users change them and quiet hours depend on when delivery happens.
- **devices**: push tokens per user. Providers report invalid tokens, which must be deleted.

<Step id="high-level" title="High-level design">

The notify API checks the idempotency key, publishes to a **durable queue** and returns. **Sender workers** consume the queue, render the template, check preferences and call the provider through a **circuit breaker** with a **bulkhead** (a cap on concurrent calls). A failed attempt leaves the message unacknowledged; the queue redelivers it after the visibility timeout and moves it to a **dead-letter queue** after five attempts.

<Step id="deep-dive" title="Deep dive: retries, breakers and priorities">

Retry transient errors (timeouts, 5xx, 429) with exponential backoff and **jitter**; never retry permanent ones such as an invalid token. Providers enforce rate limits, so senders pace themselves with a token bucket per provider. A **circuit breaker** stops hammering a failing provider; a **bulkhead** stops a slow provider from consuming every sender. Separate queues or consumer pools by **priority** so a 50M-message campaign cannot delay a 2FA code. Duplicates reaching users are suppressed per channel: APNs collapse IDs and FCM collapse keys replace an undelivered earlier notification.

<Step id="alternatives" title="Alternatives">

- **Call the provider synchronously from the API.** One less component and immediate status, but callers inherit the provider's latency and outages.
- **Separate queues per channel and priority.** More to operate, but a slow email provider can no longer delay push, and each provider's senders scale and rate-limit independently.

<Step id="failures" title="Failure scenarios you can run">

- **The provider fails half of all requests** while passing health checks. The API keeps accepting at full speed; senders retry, the breaker opens and fails fast, and redelivery delivers the messages after the incident. Watch the queue's redelivery count, not the API's goodput.
- **The provider is down long enough to fill the queue.** At first nothing changes for callers; then the 1,000-message queue fills and publishes are rejected with 503: back-pressure reaches the API. Loud and bounded is the right failure, but the queue must be sized for the longest outage you plan for.
- **The provider's latency jumps by 250 ms.** 128 senders now deliver only ~440/s against 1,000/s arriving; the queue fills and callers get 503 — latency alone produced the same back-pressure as an outage.

<Step id="wrap-up" title="Wrap up">

Summarise: idempotent accept into a durable, disk-backed queue sized for hours; senders with preferences at send time, backoff with jitter, breakers, bulkheads and provider-specific rate limits; priorities separated; DLQ for poison messages; invalid tokens removed. Signals: queue depth and age per priority, provider error rate, DLQ growth. Then score each stage from 1 to 4.
