<Step id="requirements" title="Requirements">

Upload a photo with a caption; view photos in several sizes; list a user's photos (feeds are the news feed case study). Image views p99 under ~200 ms worldwide; uploaded photos are never lost; uploads are acknowledged in a few seconds while thumbnails may appear a moment later; hundreds of views per upload.

<Step id="estimates" title="Estimates">

- **Uploads**: 100M photos/day ≈ **1,160/s**, about 3k/s at peak.
- **Storage**: 100M × (2 MB original + ~0.5 MB of resized versions) × 365 ≈ **90 PB/year**.
- **Views**: 500M daily users × 100 image views ÷ 86,400 ≈ **580k requests/s**.
- **Egress**: 580k/s × ~150 KB ≈ **87 GB/s**, about 700 Gbit/s. Only a CDN serves this economically.
- **Origin**: at a 95% CDN hit rate, 580k × 5% ≈ **29k requests/s** reach the origin. The hit rate decides the origin's size.
- **Resize workers**: Little's law, 1,160/s × 120 ms of CPU ≈ 140 cores busy on average.

<Step id="api" title="API">

- `POST /v1/media`: a resumable, chunked upload (or a pre-signed URL straight to the object store) plus the caption → **201** with the media ID and URLs, some sizes pending.
- `GET /p/{photoKey}_{size}.jpg`: served by the CDN. Keys are **immutable** — an edit creates a new key — so CDN entries never need invalidation.
- `GET /v1/users/{id}/media?cursor=` pages by media ID.

<Step id="data-model" title="Data model and IDs">

- **media**: ID, user, caption, photo key, timestamps; **sharded by user**, so a profile grid is one shard. Instagram used thousands of logical shards on PostgreSQL, mapped onto fewer physical servers.
- **photos**: image bytes in the object store under immutable keys; the database only stores the key.

Instagram generated **64-bit IDs inside PostgreSQL**: 41 bits of milliseconds since a custom epoch, 13 bits of logical shard ID, 10 bits of per-shard sequence. IDs sort by time and say which shard holds the row.

<Step id="high-level" title="High-level design">

DNS points clients at the **CDN**, with the origin as a fallback. Views are served from the CDN; misses go to the origin, which reads the **object store**. Uploads pass through to the origin, which stores the original, records metadata, and publishes a **resize job**. **Resize workers** generate the sizes and write them back to the object store.

<Step id="deep-dive" title="Deep dive: serving images">

Popularity is heavily skewed: recent popular photos hit the CDN almost every time, the long tail of old photos rarely does. An **origin shield** — a regional mid-tier cache — collapses misses from many edges into one origin read. Old photos move to cheaper, erasure-coded storage: Facebook's Haystack packed photos into large files to avoid per-file metadata reads, and f4 later moved "warm" photos to erasure-coded storage to cut replication cost.

<Step id="alternatives" title="Alternatives">

- **Serve images from your own servers.** Full control, but 700 Gbit/s of egress from a few regions is slow far away and enormously expensive.
- **Resize synchronously during upload.** Every size is ready immediately, but uploads now cost hundreds of milliseconds of CPU each and a spike saturates the API tier.
- **Run your own blob store** (Haystack, f4). Worth it at Facebook's scale; most teams should use a managed object store with lifecycle tiers.

<Step id="failures" title="Failure scenarios you can run">

- **The CDN edge goes down.** Requests fail until the DNS record expires, then the origin takes every view: object-store reads jump from the miss rate to the full rate and egress now leaves your origin. DNS TTL is your failover time.
- **Uploads spike 3×.** Uploads are acknowledged as fast as before; the resize queue grows and thumbnails lag. Scale workers on queue depth.
- **The object store slows 6×.** Most views are CDN hits and do not notice; misses slow down and the slowest exceed the API's storage timeout. The CDN's hit rate is a reliability feature, not only a cost one.
