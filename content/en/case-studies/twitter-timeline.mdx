<Step id="requirements" title="Requirements">

Post tweets; read the **home timeline** (recent tweets from followed accounts) and **user timelines** (one author's tweets). Home timeline p99 under ~200 ms; reads dominate writes by about 60:1; a tweet reaches followers within seconds — minutes for the largest accounts is not acceptable; posting never blocks on fan-out.

This builds on the news feed case study: same problem, real numbers, and the hybrid model in production.

<Step id="estimates" title="Estimates from published numbers">

Twitter's 2012 talk "Timelines at Scale" gave roughly **300k QPS** of timeline reads and about **5k tweets/s** on average (around 12k/s at peaks; about 400M tweets a day).

- **Fan-out writes**: 5k tweets/s × ~200 followers ≈ **1M timeline inserts/s**. The fan-out tier is the big write system.
- **Timeline memory**: ~150M active users × 800 tweet IDs × 8 B × 3 replicas ≈ **3 TB of RAM**; inactive users are not kept.
- **A celebrity**: an account with 30M followers is 30M inserts per tweet — seconds to minutes of fan-out.

<Step id="api" title="API">

- `POST /2/tweets` → 201 with the tweet ID.
- `GET /2/timeline/home?pagination_token=` → tweets and the next token. Because tweet IDs are time-ordered, the token is just the last ID seen.

<Step id="data-model" title="Data model">

- **tweets**: the source of truth, keyed by a **Snowflake ID** — 41 bits of milliseconds, 10 bits of worker ID, 12 bits of sequence — unique without coordination and roughly time-ordered.
- **timelines**: per-user lists of ~800 tweet IDs (with author and flags), in memory, replicated three times. Derived data: it can be rebuilt from tweets and the follow graph.
- **follow graph**: a separate service (FlockDB at the time) used by the fan-out service.

<Step id="high-level" title="High-level design: hybrid fan-out">

Posting stores the tweet and, unless the author is above the follower threshold, publishes one fan-out message. The **fan-out service** writes the tweet ID into each follower's in-memory timeline, skipping inactive users.

Reading runs two things **in parallel**: fetch the precomputed timeline (through a cache), and fetch recent tweets from the few celebrities the reader follows. Merge the two sorted lists by ID and hydrate.

<Step id="deep-dive" title="Deep dive: choosing the threshold">

Pushing an author with F followers costs F inserts per tweet, all at once. Pulling costs nothing at write time, but every timeline read by every follower pays a small query for that author, whether or not they tweeted. Push is cheaper for almost everyone; pull wins when F is so large that the burst of inserts delays everyone else's fan-out and inflates timelines that may never be read. In practice the threshold sits around accounts with millions of followers.

<Step id="alternatives" title="Alternatives">

- **Pure fan-out on write.** Every read is one lookup, but a celebrity tweet is tens of millions of writes that delay everyone else's fan-out.
- **Pure fan-out on read.** No timeline storage and instant posting, but each read gathers from hundreds of accounts across shards, with fan-out tail latency on every request.

<Step id="failures" title="Failure scenarios you can run">

- **The timeline cache is flushed.** Reads fall through to the replicated timeline store, which is sized for them: latency rises briefly and nothing fails. If timelines had to be recomputed from tweets, the same event would be an outage.
- **The fan-out service slows 6×.** Posting and reading are unaffected; consumer lag rises from near zero to a few hundred milliseconds, so new tweets appear late. Staleness instead of errors is the designed degradation.
- **A global event triples traffic.** Reads stay fast because each read is bounded work. The celebrity merge queries grow with traffic, so the tweet store's utilisation is the one to watch.
