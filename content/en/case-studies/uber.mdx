<Step id="requirements" title="Requirements">

Drivers send their GPS location every few seconds. When a rider requests a trip, the system finds nearby available drivers, offers the trip, and assigns **exactly one** driver, then tracks trip state (`requested`, `assigned`, `in_progress`, `completed`).

**Non-functional**: matching decisions complete within **~1 s**; location updates are loss-tolerant because a fresh ping arrives 4 seconds later; a driver is **never assigned two concurrent trips**; and dispatch for a city must survive a data-centre outage.

<Step id="estimates" title="Estimates">

Assume **1M online drivers** pinging every **4 seconds** and **20M trips/day**:

- **Location writes**: 1M ÷ 4 s ≈ **250,000 writes/s**, each ~100 B (≈ 75 MB/s ingress with headers).
- **Supply index memory**: 1M drivers × ~200 B (driverId, H3 cell, lat/lng, state, timestamp) × 2 replicas ≈ **400 MB** total — the entire world's live driver index fits comfortably in memory.
- **Ride requests**: 20M ÷ 86,400 × ~3 peak factor ≈ **700 requests/s** at peak — more than 300× lower than the ping rate.
- **Geo query size**: a ~2 km search in a city inspects the rider's H3 cell and 1–2 rings of neighbours, returning tens to a few hundred candidate drivers.

<Step id="api" title="API">

- `POST /v1/drivers/{id}/location` with `{ lat, lng, heading, ts }` → **200**. Idempotent with last-write-wins by device timestamp; stale out-of-order pings are dropped.
- `POST /v1/rides` with `{ pickup, dropoff, product }` and `Idempotency-Key` → **200** `{ tripId, driver }` or **409** if the candidate driver was just claimed by another request (the dispatcher retries with the next candidate).
- `POST /v1/trips/{id}/complete` → **200**, setting the driver's availability back to `1`.

<Step id="data-model" title="Data model">

- **locations** (in-memory KV store `geo`): `driverId`, `cell` (H3 resolution 8–9 hexagon), `lat`, `lng`, `updatedAt`, indexed by `cell`. Quorum is `R = 1, W = 1` over 2 replicas with hinted handoff: speed beats strict durability because the next ping arrives in 4 s.
- **drivers** (authoritative `dispatchdb`): `driverId`, `available` (`1` = free, `0` = on trip), `tripId`. Replicated semi-synchronously with fencing tokens on failover. Changed only via atomic conditional updates (`decrementIfPositive` / compare-and-set).

<Step id="high-level" title="High-level design">

Clients connect through a load balancer to stateless **Dispatch API** servers. Location pings go straight to the in-memory **Location index (`geo`)**, sharded by cell. Ride requests query `geo` by cell to get up to 30 nearby candidates, rank them by ETA, and execute an atomic `decrementIfPositive` on `available` in the **Dispatch state (`dispatchdb`)** store. Trip completion sets `available = 1`.

<Step id="deep-dive" title="Deep dive: atomic claims, leases and city failover">

1. **Assigning exactly one driver.** Read-then-write (`SELECT available` followed by `UPDATE available = 0`) has a classic race condition: two servers both read `1` and both assign the driver. Instead, use a single atomic conditional write (`UPDATE drivers SET available = 0, trip_id = :t WHERE driver_id = :d AND available = 1`, or `decrementIfPositive`). When offering a trip to a driver's phone, attach a **lease timeout** (e.g. 15 seconds) so an unanswered offer automatically releases the driver.
2. **Batch matching vs greedy.** Greedy matching claims the nearest driver immediately. Waiting 1–2 seconds to collect all requests in a neighbourhood and solving a minimum-cost bipartite matching problem reduces overall pickup ETAs when demand is dense.
3. **Regional failover.** Riders and drivers only interact within the same city, so **city is both the shard key and the failure domain**. If a data centre fails, only the cities hosted there fail over; the supply index rebuilds itself from scratch within 4 seconds of incoming driver pings.

<Step id="alternatives" title="Alternatives">

- **Read the driver's state, then write it.** The runnable `uber-unsafe-claim` alternative performs a `get` followed by an unconditional `increment(-1)`. Under the downtown surge scenario, concurrent requests read `available = 1` at the same time and double-book the driver (`oversell` violation).
- **Store locations in the relational database.** Gives ACID durability and PostGIS spatial queries, but 250k writes/s of ephemeral coordinates saturates disk I/O and starves actual trip transactions.
- **Quadtree instead of H3.** A quadtree adapts leaf sizes to density, which is great for static places (like restaurants), but constantly rebalancing a tree as a million cars drive across boundaries is much harder to shard and lock than fixed H3 hexagons.

<Step id="failures" title="Failure scenarios you can run">

- **Everyone wants the same driver downtown** (20% of traffic targets one hot driver after a concert). Exactly one claim succeeds; concurrent contenders receive **409** and move to the next candidate. No double assignment occurs.
- **Location pings triple** (a mobile app bug pings every 1 s instead of 4 s). The in-memory `geo` ring absorbs the 3× write storm in sub-millisecond time and ride matching latency barely moves.
- **The dispatch database leader dies.** Location pings keep succeeding (`geo` is separate). Ride claims and completions fail for the ~0.5 s of heartbeat detection (3 × 100 ms) and election (200 ms), then resume on the promoted replica. Semi-synchronous replication and fencing guarantee that no confirmed trip is lost or split-brained.

<Step id="wrap-up" title="Wrap up">

Summarise the design: **H3 hexagonal cells** in an in-memory store for the 250k/s ping firehose, an **authoritative transactional store** with atomic conditional claims for the 700/s dispatch decisions, and **city-scoped sharding** so a regional outage never affects other cities. Key operational metrics: **matching p99 latency**, **409 claim conflict rate** (signals hot-cell contention), and **supply index staleness**. Score yourself from 1 to 4 against the rubric.
