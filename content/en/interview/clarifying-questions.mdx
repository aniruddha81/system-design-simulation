<Step id="which-question" title="Questions that change the design">

Interview prompts are vague on purpose: discovering the real problem is part of the test. But time is short, so ask the questions whose answers would make you build something **different**. "What colour is the button?" changes nothing. "Do links ever expire?" changes the data model, storage growth and the cleanup job.

<Step id="requirements" title="Functional and non-functional">

**Functional** requirements say what the system does: create a short link, redirect, show click counts. **Non-functional** requirements say how well: redirects in under 50 ms at p99, 99.99% availability for redirects, links never lost once created. Non-functional requirements decide most of the architecture, so get numbers for them, or propose numbers and ask if they are reasonable.

<Step id="distribution" title="Ask for the distribution, not just the average">

Averages hide the cases that break systems. In a social feed, the **average** author has a few hundred followers, and fan-out on write (copying each post into every follower's timeline) looks easy. Ask the next question: *how are followers distributed?*

</Step>

<Step id="celebrity" title="The celebrity problem">

Follower counts are heavy-tailed: a few accounts have tens of millions of followers. One celebrity post under pure fan-out on write means tens of millions of timeline inserts, and followers see the post late. That is why the clarifying question matters: if the answer is "yes, there are celebrities", the standard design becomes a **hybrid**: fan out on write for ordinary authors, and merge celebrity posts into the timeline at read time.

<Step id="assumptions" title="When the answer is 'you decide'">

Interviewers often hand the decision back. Do not stall. State a reasonable assumption with a number, say why, and note what would change if it were wrong. That shows judgement, and gives the interviewer an easy way to redirect you.
