<Step id="active-passive" title="Active-passive: one region writes">

In **active-passive**, one region serves writes (and usually reads). A second region holds a replica and stands by. On a regional failure you **promote** the standby and move traffic to it.

It is simple, because there is one writer and no conflicts. Its costs: the standby's capacity is mostly idle; RPO equals the replication lag at the moment of failure (unless replication is synchronous); and RTO includes detection, the failover decision, promotion and moving traffic, where DNS TTLs and client connection pools keep some clients on the dead region for minutes.

The dangerous step is promotion. If the old primary is only partitioned rather than dead, you now have two writers. GitHub's October 2018 incident started with a 43-second partition and an automated cross-country failover; writes accepted on both sides took about a day to reconcile. Fence the old primary before promoting the new one.

<Step id="capacity" title="The survivors must carry the peak">

In **active-active**, every region serves live traffic. When one fails, the others absorb its share instantly, so there is no promotion step. But only if they have the capacity **already running**. Autoscaling during a regional failure means depending on a control plane that may itself be degraded, while every customer of that provider scales at the same moment.

AWS calls the alternative **static stability**: provision enough capacity in advance that the system keeps working through a failure without making any changes. With N regions, each must handle peak ÷ (N − 1).

<Step id="conflicts" title="Active-active writes: who wins?">

If two regions accept writes to the same record concurrently, they conflict. There are three ways out, and each costs something:

- **Home each record in one region.** A user's data is written only in their home region; other regions forward writes or serve stale reads. No conflicts, but a region failure makes its users' writes unavailable until you re-home them.
- **Merge automatically.** Last-writer-wins by timestamp is simple and silently loses one update. CRDTs (counters, sets, registers designed to merge) keep every update but fit only some data types.
- **Agree before committing.** A consensus protocol across regions, as Spanner does with Paxos, gives one serial order everywhere and costs at least one cross-region round trip per write.

<Step id="consensus-latency" title="The price of agreeing across regions">

Speed of light sets a floor: a round trip between US coasts is roughly 60 to 80 ms, and between continents often well over 100 ms. A write that must reach a majority of regions pays at least the round trip to the **nearest** replica that completes the majority, because the leader does not wait for the slowest.

That is why consensus deployments place a majority of replicas close together (for example three regions on one continent plus witnesses elsewhere), and why reads that need the latest value pay a similar cost.

<Step id="hidden-coupling" title="Shared fate across regions">

Multi-region buys independence only if nothing important is shared. Global configuration pushes, a single identity provider, one DNS provider, a global deployment pipeline and cross-region control planes can each take down every region together.
