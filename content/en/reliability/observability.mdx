<Step id="three-signals" title="Three signals, three different questions">

At 14:05 the checkout p99 doubles. You need to know three things: *is it real and how bad*, *which requests*, and *where did the time go*. Each question has a signal built for it.

- **Metrics** are numbers aggregated over time: counters, gauges and histograms per label set. They are cheap to store and fast to query, so they drive dashboards and alerts. They cannot tell you about one specific request.
- **Logs** are discrete events with arbitrary detail. A structured log line (JSON with a trace ID, user tier, error code) answers "what happened to *this* request". They cost per event, so high-traffic paths sample or drop them.
- **Traces** record the causal path of one request across services as a tree of **spans**, each with a start, a duration and a parent. Google's Dapper paper showed that a trace of a sampled fraction of requests is enough to find where latency goes in a deep call graph.

<Step id="golden-signals" title="Page on symptoms, investigate causes">

The SRE book's **four golden signals** for a user-facing system are **latency, traffic, errors and saturation**. Two conventions turn them into practice: **RED** (rate, errors, duration) for each request-driven service, and Brendan Gregg's **USE** (utilisation, saturation, errors) for each resource such as a disk, a CPU or a connection pool.

Page a human on **symptoms**, things users feel: error ratio, latency over a threshold. Use **causes** (CPU, disk, queue depth) on dashboards and tickets. A cause-based page fires when nothing is wrong for users, and stays silent for failures nobody predicted.

Two traps: measure the latency of **errors separately**, because a fast 500 drags the average down and hides an outage; and use **histograms**, not averages, because you cannot average percentiles across hosts.

<Step id="cardinality" title="Labels multiply">

A metric's cost is its number of **time series**: one per distinct combination of label values. Labels multiply. A histogram with 20 endpoints, 5 status classes and 100 pods is already 10,000 combinations, and each one stores a series per bucket.

That is why IDs never go in metric labels. A `user_id` label on a service with a million users creates a million combinations per metric and can take down the metrics backend. Per-request detail belongs in logs and traces; link them to metrics with **exemplars** (a trace ID attached to a histogram bucket sample).

<Step id="cardinality-fix" title="Which labels are safe?">

A safe label has a small, **bounded** set of values that does not grow with traffic or users. Normalise unbounded values before they become labels: the route template `/users/:id`, not the raw path; the status class `5xx`, not the error message.

<Step id="context" title="Follow the trace context">

A trace survives service hops because every call carries **trace context**. The W3C `traceparent` header holds a version, a 128-bit trace ID shared by the whole request, the ID of the calling span, and flags whose lowest bit says whether this trace is **sampled**. For example: `00-4bf92f3577b34da6a3ce929d0e0e4736-00f067aa0ba902b7-01`.

Each service starts a child span whose parent is the incoming span ID, and passes its own span ID downstream. If one hop drops the header, for example a message queue or a proxy that strips unknown headers, the trace breaks into disconnected fragments.

<Step id="sampling" title="Sample, and budget what you keep">

Traces are the most expensive signal per request: one request can produce dozens of spans. Two families of sampling control the cost.

- **Head-based** sampling decides at the root, for example keeping 1% of traces, and propagates the decision. It is cheap and consistent, but it keeps 1% of your rare errors too.
- **Tail-based** sampling buffers every span until the trace completes, then keeps the interesting ones: errors, slow requests, rare routes. It catches what matters but needs a collector tier that holds every span for the length of the slowest trace.

Estimate before you turn tracing on. The volume is requests × sampling fraction × spans per trace × bytes per span.
