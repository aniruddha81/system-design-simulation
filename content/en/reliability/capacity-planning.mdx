<Step id="demand" title="Plan for the peak you will have">

Capacity planning answers one question ahead of time: **when does demand exceed what we can serve, and what do we buy or build before then?** Plan against **peak**, not average: the daily peak, the weekly peak, and the event peak (a launch, a sale, a holiday). Then add the capacity that failures and maintenance take away.

Demand has two parts: **organic growth**, which you forecast from history, and **inorganic** steps such as launches, migrations and new customers, which you get from the product calendar.

<Step id="growth" title="Compounding growth arrives sooner">

Growth compounds. A service growing 8% per month is about 2.5× bigger after a year. Linear extrapolation underestimates how soon you hit the wall, and provisioning has lead time: hardware orders, region build-outs and re-sharding take months. Work backwards from the saturation date.

<Step id="headroom" title="Why you never plan for 100%">

Two forces eat into capacity.

**Queueing.** As utilisation ρ approaches 1, waiting time explodes. For a simple single-server queue, mean response time is the service time ÷ (1 − ρ): twice the service time at 50%, five times at 80%, ten times at 90%. Your latency SLO breaks long before CPU reads 100%.

**Loss of capacity.** A zone or region fails, a rolling deploy has a batch out, a node is draining. With N + 1 or N + 2 planning, the surviving capacity must still meet peak at an acceptable utilisation.

<Step id="breaking-point" title="The first thing to break">

As traffic grows, components saturate one at a time. The one with the highest utilisation per unit of traffic goes first, and its failure often changes the load on everything else: a saturated database slows responses, timeouts trigger retries, and retries raise load on the service tier too. Find the first rung before you find out in production.

<Step id="sweep" title="Sweep the load in Scale mode">

Arithmetic gives the first rung. A **breaking-point sweep** gives the whole ladder. In the sandbox's **Scale** tab, a sweep runs the same design at up to twelve load levels, log-spaced from 1× to 1000× the selected load, in a fluid model fast enough for planet-scale traffic. It then shows the **failure ladder**: the predicted order in which components saturate, overflow queues or lose timely success, with the load multiple of each rung.

It has two kinds, and they answer different questions:

- **Independent levels**: each level starts fresh after a warm-up, so caches, autoscaling and queues are reset. This answers "what can a warmed-up system sustain at this load?"
- **Continuous ramp**: state carries over between levels. This exposes what history causes: retry storms, autoscaling lag and cache churn.

Each rung offers a remedy to try. It is only a suggestion until a what-if run with the remedy actually improves the ladder; the panel marks it **verified** only then.
