<Step id="sli" title="Measure what users feel">

An **SLI** is a ratio: **good events ÷ valid events**. The art is choosing the events and the boundary. Measure as close to the user as you can: load balancer logs see requests your servers never logged because they crashed; client-side telemetry also sees DNS and network failures, but is noisier.

For latency, do not use an average. Use the **proportion of requests faster than a threshold**: "99% of valid requests are served in under 300 ms". It is a ratio like availability, so it has a budget, and it composes correctly across hosts, which percentiles do not.

<Step id="target" title="100% is the wrong target">

The SRE book's argument: a user on a 99% reliable phone cannot tell 99.99% from 99.999%, because their own device, Wi-Fi and ISP fail far more often. Meanwhile cost rises steeply: each increment of reliability can cost far more than the previous one. A **100% SLO** also leaves no room to ship, since any change risks a failure.

Pick the target from what users need and what the system has achieved, then keep a slightly stricter **internal SLO** than any external **SLA**, so you notice trouble before you owe credits.

<Step id="budget" title="Turn the SLO into a budget">

The **error budget** is 1 − SLO, applied to the window. For a good-minutes SLO of 99.9% over 30 days, that is 30 × 1,440 × 0.001 = **43.2 bad minutes**. For a request-based SLO, it is a number of failed requests instead, and it burns faster at peak.

A **rolling** window (the last 28 or 30 days) changes smoothly; a **calendar** window resets on the 1st, which can encourage risky launches on the last day.

<Step id="burn-rate" title="Burn rate: how fast is the budget going?">

The **burn rate** is the observed error ratio divided by the budgeted error ratio. At burn rate 1 you spend exactly the whole budget over the window. At burn rate 14.4, a 30-day budget lasts about two days, and one hour consumes 2% of it.

</Step>

<Step id="alerting" title="Alert on burn rate, over two windows">

A static threshold such as "error rate above 1%" is either too noisy for a loose SLO or too slow for a strict one. The SRE Workbook instead pages on **fast burn** and tickets on **slow burn**, for a 30-day budget:

- page when **2%** of the budget burns in **1 hour** (burn rate 14.4) or **5%** in **6 hours** (burn rate 6);
- open a ticket when **10%** burns in **3 days** (burn rate 1).

Each long window is paired with a **short window** (5 minutes for the 1-hour alert, 30 minutes for the 6-hour alert). Both must exceed the threshold, so the alert stops firing soon after the problem is fixed, instead of staying red for an hour while the long window drains.

<Step id="policy" title="Let the budget decide">

An error budget turns "move fast" versus "be careful" into arithmetic both sides agreed on in advance. A typical **error budget policy**: while budget remains, teams ship at their own pace; when it is exhausted, feature launches pause except for reliability fixes and security patches until the window recovers; a single incident that burns a large share of the budget gets a postmortem.

The policy must be written and signed by product and engineering before the bad month, not negotiated during it.
